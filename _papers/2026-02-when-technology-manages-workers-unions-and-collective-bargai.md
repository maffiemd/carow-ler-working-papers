abstract: >-
  Artificial intelligence and digital technologies are transforming work, but
  their consequences for workers and unions remain unclear. We distinguish
  displacement, augmentation, and monitoring technologies and argue that each
  reshapes job stability, workplace interaction, and union leverage differently.
  Using survey evidence from workers in Western countries, we show that
  augmentation and monitoring are associated with higher unionization, whereas
  displacement predicts the reverse, reflecting how different technologies
  reshape working conditions. We then analyze a novel dataset we assembled of
  more than 40,000 Canadian collective bargaining agreements from 1993 to 2025,
  showing that exposure shapes not only membership but also what unions bargain
  for. Augmentation and monitoring are linked to stronger protections on
  training, governance, notice, and surveillance, while displacement is
  associated with weaker protections. Together, these findings suggest that the
  future of work is not predetermined by technology alone but is also negotiated
  at the bargaining table.
